Biruni's Measurement of the Earth
Alberto Gomez Gomez1

Abstract
The peak from which Biruni measured the Earth is pinpointed; his measurements are shown to tally with the
geographical features of the landscape around the peak. Mathematics shows what measurements he is likely to have
taken, and his famous formula is fully derived. Also, an appendix is given for those who wish to measure mountains
by themselves and follow in Biruni’s footsteps.
Keywords: Biruni, Nandana, Earth’s radius, Baghanwala, Masudic Canon, Hakimite Tables, al-Mamun, Salt Range, Sine Law

1. Introduction
Abu Rayhan (Muhammad ibn Ahmad) al-Biruni
was born in Kath, capital of Khwarezm (the region
of the Amu Darya delta), in modern Khiva, on
September 4, 973 (362-12-03 AH), and died in
Ghazni, Khorasan, on December 13, 1048 (44007-04 AH).1 He was a well-travelled Persian sage
who spoke fluent Persian, Arabic, Turkish, Sanskrit,
Greek, Hebrew, Syrian, and several other local languages in whose literatures he was also well versed.
Not only did he possess ‘all the well known books
on astronomy written within the area extending
from the Mediterranean Sea to the Bay of Bengal, including all the Greek, Indian, and Muslim
authors from Spain to Egypt’ (Biruni, 1956:A13;
Ahmad, 2010:171), but he also wrote prolifically
on all branches of knowledge, like cartography,
geography, ethnography, history, philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, and science in general, a total
of about 180 works, some sadly lost to us now (EI2,
1979:A1237). Chief among these is his Masudic
Canon, an encyclopaedia of astronomy he dedicated
to Sultan Masud, who, delighted with the sage’s
accomplishment, presented him with an elephantload of silver! To everyone’s amazement, Biruni
refused it, pleading that he could not bear to abuse
the generosity of someone who had already shown
him more kindnesses than he felt he deserved (Siraj,
1975:343-4).
1Some think that, according to the solar calendar, the Muslim date

Of his many contributions to the good of humanity, it is hard to decide which is the most important,
and certainly it is not in the scope of a short paper
like this to do justice to the vastness of his genius.
So, it is as a matter of personal choice that the
author decided to deal here with just one of these:
the fact that he measured the Earth with an unprecedented precision, not equalled in the West until
the 16th century (Norhudzaev, 1973). You may
find the author is rather critical sometimes, for,
in Biruni’s own teachings, it is the truth that we
must seek and speak, even when it goes against
us. His value for the Earth’s radius (6335.725 km)
was the result of having solved a complex geodesic
equation, as we will see. But to fully understand
the whole story, we must know something about its
background and about the early Islamic measures
then (and herein) used: these were exactly those
of ancient Mesopotamia, where ‘each mile was a
third of a farsang, or 4000 cubits, called ‘black’
in Iraq, each of which equalled 24 digits’ (Biruni,
1934:208; Wright, 1934:119; Mercier, 1993:177).
That is, one farsang (5916 m) equalled three miles,
one mile (1972 m) equalled 4000 cubits, and one
(black) cubit (493 mm) equalled twenty-four digits.
Note the connection with the Roman mile (1479 m)
of 3000 cubits (each of 493 mm).

5. Exactly how exact?
Armed with his newly found formula and with
the data he had taken at Nandana (namely, a
height of 652.055 cubits, and a dip of 34′ ), he
stated that the Earth’s radius was 12,851,369.845
cubits (6335.725 km). Had he had a calculator to
hand, he would have got 13,331,728.352 cubits
(6572.542 km) instead, but in his time, calculations
were performed in a rather mental way. Instead
of using cos (34′ ), he used sin ( 90 ◦ − 34 ′ ) , that
is, sin (89◦ 26′ ), which is very much the same, but
for the fact that it was then also fashionable to
express numbers in the old Babylonian way, and the
value he gives in his Masudic Canon for this sine
is 0◦ 59′ 59 ′′ 9 ′′′2′′′′ 28′′′′′, which is slightly inaccurate. The correct number (as given by modern
computers and expressed in the same historical
fashion) should have been 0◦59 ′ 59 ′′49′′′ 26′′′′ 9 ′′′′′,
hence the disagreement. He then went on to find
the Earth’s girth, which he stated to be 80,780,039
cubits 1′ 33′′ (clearly, the result of rounding 𝜋 up
to 22/7).
6. Biruni’s Peak
Let us now readdress the issue of the mountain. We
have already mentioned how Mercier (1993:183)
pinpointed it as likely to be one standing about
1.4 km south-southwest of the fort and 478 m above
sea level. The author has not found such a place.

Paper ID: SR23326005322

Instead, he found a mountain 780 m southwest of the
fort (N32◦ 43′ 14.60′′ , E73◦13 ′ 26.00′′ ) that stands
533 m above sea level. Since Biruni said ‘west,’
the author also checked another candidate 3.8 km
west of the fort (N32 ◦ 43′ 46.08′′, E73◦ 11′ 20′′ ) and
standing 677 m above sea level. From them, the
plain to the south looks strikingly flat. It is hard
to imagine a flatter thing, or one better suited for
Biruni’s purpose. Yet, of course, it cannot be as flat
as the sea.
Slowly and somewhat unevenly, it slopes away
to the south getting higher and then lower than at
the mountain’s foot. In Figure 3, you can see how
the plain evolves as seen from the 533 m peak: it
first sinks to about 202 m above sea level, then rises
again to about 211 or 212 m, and then resumes
sloping away for as far as the eye can see. All this
is important because, if you take the mountain’s
height and take the higher of the plain’s levels
from it, you find how much the mountain stands
above these levels, that is, 533 − 212 = 321 m or
652 cubits, which is Biruni’s figure exactly! But it
wouldn’t be wise to measure a mountain from this
far away. If you come closer and allow Biruni to
take his measurements from the area between 21/2
and 31/2 km south of our peak and gently sloping
from about 212 to 211 m above sea level, you get
again 652 cubits exactly. The choice of this area
has the advantage not only of being close enough
to allow reliable measurements, but also, and above
all, of being on a level with the apparent horizon,
which is seen from it as the plain’s upper levels.
Seemingly, this was Biruni’s logical way of finding
the ‘plain’s [otherwise relative] level.’
Measuring the dip of the horizon from the Salt
Range tops is easier said than done. As Rizvi
(1979:619) reports, it takes a good deal of patience
to wait for the right atmospheric conditions to get
a clear view of the horizon. It is best to try after
rain has cleared away the haze and dust from the air.
(It is possible that Biruni lived in rainier times, for,
after all, there must be some reason why the name
Nandana means ‘Paradise’ in Sanskrit.) Even so,
the dip he reports, 34′ , though strikingly accurate,
is not given to seconds of a degree, and therefore
does not allow us to use mathematics this time
to find out whether he averaged it out of several
measurements or not. Perhaps the prevailing poor
visibility forced him to be satisfied with fewer measurements, or perhaps he had some other reason for
the figure he gave.
